title: "Does Zone Size Matter? A Scoping Review of Ex-post Evaluations of Urban Congestion Charges and Low Emission Zones"
author: "Hyesop Shin (School of Environment, University of Auckland)"
date: "Draft 3 (scoping review), 2 November 2026"

**Abstract**

Urban congestion charges and low emission zones range from a single street to a whole metropolis. This scoping review charted 115 ex-post estimates from 61 studies against an inventory of 50 zone-based schemes. Only 29 schemes had a published evaluation, 11 of 30 scheme phases had a controlled estimate of their main outcome, and a peer-reviewed source stated the zone area for only six. Reported effects showed no consistent relationship with zone size. Traffic reductions from charges largely persisted, but only six phases had repeated estimates from one source. Evaluations should report zone geometry and measurement location.

**Keywords:** congestion charging, low emission zone, zone size, scoping review, air quality, road pricing

# 1. Questions

Urban access schemes range from 0.35 km² to over 1,500 km². Reviews have grouped their effects by scheme type or health outcome (Chamberlain et al., 2023, Holman et al., 2015), but none of those we screened treated zone size or time in operation as a dimension of the evidence. We ask three questions.

1. How much ex-post evidence exists for zone-based congestion charges and low emission zones (LEZs), and of what design?
2. How do evaluations report zone size, measurement location and time in operation?
3. Do reported effects show any pattern with zone size or years in operation?

# 2. Methods

We followed JBI guidance (Peters et al., 2020) and report against PRISMA-ScR (Tricco et al., 2018, Supplementary S5). The protocol was registered on OSF after the scheme-by-scheme search and before the database searches [registration DOI to add].

**Inventory.** From a compiled database of 65 urban road charging and access schemes we removed 28 that had no bounded zone or were duplicates, and added 13 schemes with known evaluations, giving 50 zone-based schemes (Supplementary S1).

**Search and selection.** We searched Scopus, TRID and Web of Science [date and counts to add] with strings combining scheme terms, evaluation designs and traffic or pollution outcomes (Supplementary S2). Scheme-by-scheme Google Scholar searches (October and November 2026) and the reference lists of three reviews supplemented these. Two reviewers screened records independently in Covidence [kappa to add]. We included peer-reviewed ex-post quantitative estimates of change in traffic or ambient pollution. Where none existed for a scheme we included conference papers, non-indexed journal articles or council monitoring reported in a peer-reviewed review, and flagged them.

**Charting.** For each estimate we charted the outcome, effect, measurement location, years in operation, design and spillover. Design was charted in four types (untreated control or natural experiment, adjusted before-after, raw before-after, model) as a descriptor rather than a quality score. Estimates reported only as not significant, without a point estimate, were charted as zero (four LEZs). For each scheme we charted the zone area stated in a peer-reviewed article (usually an included evaluation), and separately the area from official documents or computed from official or OpenStreetMap polygons, so that reporting could itself be mapped. Schemes whose boundary changed were split into phases (Supplementary S3). Relative coverage is the area of the zone in which the effect was measured divided by its urban centre area (European Commission, Joint Research Centre, 2024).

**Synthesis.** Evidence is mapped by scheme type, design, outcome and reporting. For question 3 we took one headline estimate per scheme phase (earliest duration band, then best design, then the median) and describe patterns with medians and Spearman rank correlations, without inferential claims.

# 3. Findings

**3.1 Evidence base**

Of the 50 inventory schemes, 29 had an ex-post estimate. All 13 congestion charges were evaluated, against 15 of 33 emission zones and one of four access restrictions. These shares are inflated because eight charges were added for having known evaluations, and only 16 of the 37 schemes in the source database had an estimate. Nine of the 18 British schemes were evaluated, and none of the seven schemes in China, Latin America, Indonesia, France or Poland. Eleven of the 30 scheme phases had a control group or natural experiment for their main outcome (two of 13 charges and nine of 17 LEZs), although 18 had one for some outcome. Six charging schemes rested on conference papers, council monitoring, a secondary citation or a non-indexed journal. Charges were evaluated mostly on traffic and LEZs on NO₂ and PM10. Only three LEZs had traffic estimates.

**3.2 Reporting of size, location and time**

A peer-reviewed article stated the zone area for only six of the 30 phases. Eight areas came from official figures, ten were computed from official or OpenStreetMap boundary polygons, and four Nordic cordons could not be recovered (Table 1). Compiled areas for Stockholm ranged from 24 to 47 km². Charge evaluations mixed cordon crossings with traffic inside the zone, and 12 phases reported any change outside the boundary. The median headline estimate referred to about six months after launch. Only six phases had repeated estimates from the same source, and four (with the pooled German panels) had an estimate four or more years in.

**3.3 Patterns with size and time**

Reported effects showed no consistent relationship with zone size (Figure 1). Four LEZ evaluations found no detectable NO₂ effect, three of them in zones covering more than 15% of the urban centre. With these charted as zero, larger LEZs tended to report smaller reductions (ρ = 0.37, n = 17), but without them there was no pattern (ρ = 0.16, n = 13). London's central zone cut roadside NO₂ by about 19%, but the London-wide expansion, launched when 93% of vehicles already complied, had no detectable effect (Tong et al., 2025). For charges the direction depended on the measure (ρ = −0.58 with relative coverage, 0.68 with km², n = 9), and the km² pattern rested on three sources outside indexed journals (Durham, Singapore and Valletta). Cordons displaced traffic to their edges in Milan (Gibson & Carnovale, 2015) and Gothenburg (Börjesson & Kristoffersson, 2015).

Where repeated, charge effects largely persisted (Figure 2). The ratio of latest to earliest estimate was 1.04 for Stockholm five and a half years after the trial began (Börjesson & Kristoffersson, 2018), 1.21 for London (Givoni, 2012) and 0.88 for Gothenburg (Börjesson & Kristoffersson, 2018). Milan's Ecopass, which charged by emission class, fell to 0.76, which Danielis et al. (2012) attribute to the shrinking charged fleet. Munich's NO₂ estimate moved from +2% to −10% over five years as standards tightened (Gu et al., 2022).

**3.4 Summary**

The evidence base is thin, concentrated in Europe and dominated by short-run before-after estimates, and evaluations rarely state the size of the zone they evaluate. Zone size did not consistently predict reported effects. One untested explanation is that averages over larger zones include less polluted areas. Charting was by one reviewer with a second checking a sample, and several values were converted or taken from summaries (Supplementary S6). Evaluations should report zone area and population covered, measurement location, compliance at launch and repeated estimates at fixed intervals.
